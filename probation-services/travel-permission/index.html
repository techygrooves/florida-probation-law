Travel Permission While on Probation

Leaving the county or the state while under Florida supervision normally requires permission in advance. This page is about getting that permission for a specific trip.

On this page

  1. What travel permission covers
  2. When this comes up
  3. What the supervising authority may consider
  4. The general process
  5. Information that helps a request
  6. Travel is not relocation

What travel permission covers

Standard conditions of Florida probation typically restrict movement outside a defined area without approval. In practice that means a trip out of county, and almost always a trip out of state, needs to be authorised before you go.

This is a permission request about a specific journey — where, when, why, and back by when. It is not a change to the sentence, and getting it granted does not alter any other condition.

When this comes up

  • Work travel — a job that requires crossing county or state lines, regularly or occasionally.
  • Family emergencies — illness or a funeral, where the trip is urgent and unplanned.
  • Medical treatment unavailable locally.
  • Family obligations — a custody arrangement or child-related commitment in another county.
  • Vacation — usually the hardest to justify, and the most likely to depend on your record.

Travelling without approval is a technical violation

Leaving the permitted area without permission can be treated as a violation of supervision regardless of the reason for the trip. An emergency does not authorise itself — contact your officer before you go, even at short notice, and keep a record that you did.

What the supervising authority may consider

Most routine travel requests are handled by the supervising officer rather than the court, and the considerations are practical:

  • Your reporting and compliance history.
  • Whether the purpose is documented — a letter, an itinerary, an appointment.
  • How long you will be away and whether you will miss a scheduled appointment.
  • Whether restitution and supervision fees are current.
  • Whether any condition of your sentence restricts travel specifically.
  • How much notice you have given.

Some requests need a court order rather than officer approval — longer absences, travel that conflicts with a condition, or foreign travel. Which route applies depends on your order and your county.

The general process

  1. Check the written conditions for anything addressing travel.
  2. Ask the supervising officer well in advance, with the specifics.
  3. Supply supporting documentation with the request rather than after it.
  4. Where officer approval is not sufficient, a motion is filed with the sentencing court.
  5. Carry the written permission with you, and keep to its terms.

Notice is the single most useful thing you control. A request made three weeks ahead with an itinerary attached is a different proposition from one made the day before.

Information that helps a request

  • Exact dates of departure and return.
  • Destination address and how to reach you while away.
  • Purpose, with documentation: employer letter, appointment confirmation, funeral notice, itinerary.
  • Method of travel.
  • Confirmation that no appointment is missed, or a plan to reschedule it.

Travel is not relocation

This page is about going somewhere and coming back. Changing where you live is a different request with different requirements — see relocation for a move within Florida, and interstate transfer for moving supervision to another state.

Common questions

How much notice should I give?

As much as you can. There is no universal requirement, but requests made well ahead with documentation attached are easier to approve than last-minute ones. Ask your officer what their office expects.

What about an emergency I could not plan for?

Contact your officer before travelling if there is any way to do so, and document the attempt. Leaving first and explaining later risks a violation even where the emergency was genuine.

Can I travel internationally?

Foreign travel is treated more restrictively than domestic and generally involves the court rather than only the supervising officer. Treat it as a question needing individual advice well in advance.

My job needs constant travel. Is there a longer-term answer?

Possibly. A modification can address a recurring travel requirement rather than handling each trip separately. That is a court request, and the case for it is stronger with an employer letter.
